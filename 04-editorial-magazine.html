Cover Story · Infrastructure

The case for the
small, honest
server.

In which a small Luxembourg shop, founded in a Tampa apartment, has been quietly running KVM slices for fourteen years — without acquisitions, without rebrandings, and, more remarkably, without raising prices.

By The BuyVM Desk 14 min read Photographs by The Rack, Floor 3
Rack 7, BuyVM Luxembourg. Photographed in available light, May 2026.

Most cloud companies are five years old. Ours has been answering the same support email address since the Obama administration.

It is, on the face of it, a strange time to be selling cheap servers. The hyperscalers have spent a decade convincing the market that infrastructure should be metered to the millisecond, billed in arrears, and delivered through a console with seven hundred icons. The result is a generation of engineers who have never typed apt-get install nginx on a machine they actually own.

BuyVM has been doing the unfashionable thing instead. Two-dollar slices. Full-root KVM. A flat monthly bill. The same control panel since Drew Carey was hosting the Price Is Right.

It works. It has worked for fourteen years. It will, the management insists, continue to work.

The arithmetic is unsentimental. A small KVM slice with one virtual core, half a gigabyte of memory, and ten gigabytes of solid-state disk costs two American dollars per month. The same machine on a hyperscaler costs, at this writing, between fifteen and forty — depending on which sub-product you select and how much of the pricing page you read.

What you are paying for, in the more expensive case, is a sales organization, a marketing organization, a regulatory affairs office, and a quarterly call. What you are paying for at BuyVM is a rack and the people who maintain it.

This is not, to be clear, an argument that hyperscalers are bad. They are excellent for the workloads they were built for. They are also expensive, opaque, and prone to surprising you with a bill in the third week of February.

The argument is smaller and more boring: there exists a class of work — a Mastodon instance, a personal mail server, a build runner, a cache, a side project — for which the right answer is a small Linux box with a public IP, a sensible price, and a phone number that goes to a person who knows what BGP is.

That box is what the rest of this issue is about.

“The price you pay in the second year is the same price you paid in the first. We have receipts going back to 2010.” — Frantech engineering, on its policy of not raising prices

Layer-3 and Layer-4 mitigation on every IP we issue. The same equipment we use to keep our own panel up — pointed at your slice — at no additional charge, ever, for any plan, including the Piglet.

One gigabit,
unmetered.

No bandwidth meter ticking down. Pull a Linux ISO every hour. Mirror Debian. Host a backup. We measured the link, not your packets, and we charge for the former.

You get a virtual machine, not a container with marketing on top. Bring your own kernel, your own iptables, your own questionable Tuesday-night decisions. We’ll be here in the morning.

Interview

“We have not raised prices since 2010.”

A short conversation with Frantech engineering, conducted over IRC, lightly edited for clarity and against our better judgment.

Q.

Most providers add a sub-product every quarter. You haven’t. Why?

A.

Because most of those sub-products are accounting, not engineering. We sell virtual machines, block storage, and DDoS filtering. That covers about ninety-five percent of what people on a small budget actually need. The rest is theatre.

Q.

And the prices?

A.

The hardware gets cheaper every year. Our overhead doesn’t. Net of the two, the math has worked out about the same since 2010, so we’ve left it alone. The two-dollar slice still costs two dollars. We have customers on plans we no longer technically sell — we don’t kick them off.

Q.

Last question. Why “a touch of insanity”?

A.

Because at some point a person has to acknowledge that running a hosting company in 2026 with the same prices as 2010 is, on its face, a slightly mad thing to do. We are at peace with that.
